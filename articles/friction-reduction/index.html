We Are Choosing By Not Choosing PART 1 / 5

PART 1 — THE FRICTION PRINCIPLE

The Friction Reduction Principle Always Wins

People reach for whatever removes the obstacle in front of them. This creates genuine value, and a default path that leads somewhere whether we are paying attention or not.

10 min read

AI creates genuine value. Customer service chatbots resolve routine inquiries in seconds. Document synthesis that once took hours now takes minutes. First drafts appear before writers finish their coffee. These gains are real, and organizations pursuing them are acting rationally.

The question is not whether to adopt AI. That debate ended. The question is whether organizations are choosing their path or simply following the one that offers least resistance.

I spent Fall 2025 interviewing seven experts who navigate these decisions at the highest levels of organizational practice. They work at McKinsey advising Fortune 500 clients, at Microsoft AI building products for billions, at Carnegie Mellon studying human-AI complementarity, at Steelcase rethinking workplace futures, at Blink UX prototyping AI experiences, at IA Collaborative designing healthcare innovations, and at Doblin after decades building the innovation field. None of them oppose AI adoption. All of them use these tools daily. What they share is a commitment to deploying AI with intention rather than momentum.

Across industries and roles, they observed the same pattern: organizations optimize for friction reduction without asking what friction protects. Each individual decision makes sense. The aggregate creates consequences that productivity metrics miss.

This series offers frameworks for leaders who want to choose deliberately. Not frameworks against AI, but frameworks for the human judgment that makes AI deployment wise rather than merely fast. The goal is healthy skepticism, not resistance. The opportunity is intentional design, not default acceptance.

The Pull of Frictionless

Friction reduction operates as a first principle in technology adoption. Remove the obstacle. Eliminate the step. Automate the task. This principle created the graphical user interface, the smartphone, the one-click purchase. It works.

"Technology tools alone will never fundamentally change the way work is done," cautions Ewan Duncan, a senior partner at McKinsey who has guided design practices from IDEO through Doblin to one of the world's largest consultancies. "Tools are tools. You need a clear aspiration of what you're trying to achieve. Then you insert AI." But clarity about aspiration arrives slowly. Friction reduction arrives immediately.

The default path forms through this asymmetry. Leaders face pressure to demonstrate AI adoption. They see competitors announcing deployments. They hear promises of productivity gains. The friction reduction principle offers immediate rewards: faster reports, automated summaries, generated first drafts. The costs remain invisible.

Kelly Franznick, Chief Innovation Officer and Co-founder of Blink UX, observes this pattern across his client base of large technology companies and startups. "Companies are taking a low-hanging fruit approach," he explains, "saying where can we have the most cost savings with the least risk and using that as their first AI forays." Customer service goes first. Then internal documentation. Then anything that looks like a vendor relationship that might be replaced.

This approach makes sense as individual decisions. Each deployment reduces friction. Each automation delivers measurable returns. The problem emerges only when you step back and ask what these decisions create in aggregate.

The Moving Target

Organizations assume the line between AI capability and human necessity holds still. It moves constantly.

"That's going to be a constantly moving target," observes Donna Flynn, Chief People Officer at Steelcase, describing how her organization thinks about human-AI boundaries. "It's going to keep getting better and better. But we're just going to have to do that dance over time and figure out how to continually be aiming ourselves where we know that AI is not going to perform to what we need."

The constant movement creates what Ken Holstein, Assistant Professor at Carnegie Mellon's Human-Computer Interaction Institute, calls temporal optimism. When researchers and practitioners define what remains "irreducibly human," they make bets. Some things AI cannot do now. Some things fall within a "practical window" of development. Some things AI may never do. The boundaries between these categories shift weekly.

"Different researchers place their bets differently about what will remain irreducibly human, or at least what will remain resistant to full automation," Holstein explains. His research on human-AI complementarity spans child welfare systems, healthcare, and education. In each domain, he watches the same pattern: assumptions about human necessity prove temporary.

This moving target creates a specific problem for organizational planning. If leaders wait to act until AI reaches human capability, they wait too long. If they preserve human involvement in tasks AI masters, they face accusations of inefficiency. The default path offers a seductive solution: follow capability as it advances, reduce friction wherever possible, and trust that humans will remain necessary somewhere.

What Nobody Measures

The default path generates ripples that organizational metrics miss entirely.

Kathleen Brandenburg, Principal at IA Collaborative, offers an analogy from physical fitness. "If you use ChatGPT, and maybe you're already noticing this yourself, it's just like going to the gym," she explains. "When we're proxying information over and over and over again, eventually our own brains, they atrophy. It's more difficult to write a Monday note today than it ever has been before, because I am proxying all the things that I don't want my brain using."

Brandenburg identifies this as an issue of human agency and control. "Everyone is very aware of and concerned with losing control, losing agency, losing power," she notes. "Early adopters of AI tools are recognizing, whoa, I'm struggling to write a simple email." These are personal observations. Productivity dashboards ignore them. Quarterly reviews overlook the slow erosion of capability that comes from outsourcing cognitive work.

The ripples extend beyond individual cognition into organizational structures that took decades to build. Larry Keeley, who co-founded Doblin and shepherded it through 35 years of growth before it joined Deloitte, described the ecosystem his firm once created: "Ripples in the pond, how many different design firms were transformed, teachers came out of it, three separate spinoffs for social science research firms." That ecosystem required scale. It required hiring cohorts of young professionals, training them, watching them leave to start their own practices. None of that ecosystem-building would happen today, Keeley argued, because AI enables small teams to do work that once required hundreds. The economics have inverted.

"Nobody's hiring their students all of a sudden," Keeley observed about conversations with professors at business schools worldwide. When he presents on AI to MBA candidates, students nod along with enthusiasm. The professors at the back of the room react differently. They recognize what the students miss: these tools eliminate the entry-level positions that once absorbed graduates.

This dynamic resembles a classic collective action problem. Each student who masters AI tools gains individual advantage. Each firm that automates entry-level work gains efficiency. But the collective infrastructure that produces new professionals depends on those entry-level positions. Keeley's observation raises a question organizations must answer: if everyone optimizes individually, who builds the next generation of practitioners?

The Accountability Gap

Leaders are held accountable for their decisions. This principle anchors corporate governance, regulatory frameworks, and organizational trust. AI deployment complicates it fundamentally.

Flynn raises the question directly: "Are we going to let machines make all our decisions? How is the SEC going to hold machines accountable? How are shareholders going to hold machines accountable if a company stops making money and isn't profitable?" These structures assume human decision-makers. They assume someone chose, someone judged, someone can explain. AI diffuses responsibility across human-machine boundaries in ways existing governance cannot trace.

Flynn offers a concrete example from her volunteer work outside Steelcase. A nonprofit needed to hire an executive director. A board member decided ChatGPT should write the job description. "He got this 1,700-word job description," Flynn recounts. "There's some good color in here, there's some good ideas, but it's too long. It doesn't clearly define what the job is. It doesn't define the context of why you want this right now. And it's repetitive."

The board planned to use it anyway. Flynn intervened. "He didn't have healthy skepticism of the output," she diagnosed. "We need to have healthy skepticism and be the human in the loop."

Healthy skepticism requires expertise. You must know enough about executive director roles to recognize when 1,700 words fail to define one. You must understand nonprofit governance to notice missing context. You must have written job descriptions before to identify repetition. The default path creates a circular problem: it removes the practice that builds expertise while increasing reliance on expertise to evaluate AI outputs.

Holstein's research on AI in child welfare systems illuminates this dynamic. Caseworkers using predictive models face what his team calls "information asymmetries between humans and machines." The model knows administrative data: ages, prior system contacts, jail records. The caseworker knows the actual allegations, the reasons for the phone call, the family context. "One example of something that humans have access to is basically any knowledge about what the allegations are, what's going on, why was there even a phone call to child welfare services," Holstein explains. "That's a pretty huge blind spot for the model."

Expertise enables the caseworker to weigh these unobservables against the model's recommendation. Remove that expertise, and the model's blind spots become the organization's blind spots.

Seams and Friction as Features

The alternative to frictionless automation exists in design theory. It requires deliberate effort.

Liz Danzico, a design leader at Microsoft AI, recalls the work of Mark Weiser, the Xerox PARC researcher who coined the term "ubiquitous computing" in the late 1980s.2 Weiser advocated for systems with "beautiful seams"1 rather than seamless experiences, and later argued that technologists carry responsibility for the social changes their work produces.3 The seams reveal how technology works. They create productive friction.

"Right now I think one of the problems is that there are no seams," Danzico observes. In the early days of responsible AI design at Microsoft, "one of the responsible AI team's advice to all of us as we were designing these experiences was to make sure to put that friction in, so people were aware of the interaction with AI at each stage."

Intentional friction sounds inefficient. The opposite is true. Friction creates awareness. Awareness enables judgment. Judgment produces better outcomes than blind speed.

Duncan articulates the leadership challenge this creates: "Very few people are asking what should we apply AI to. It's at a level above 'what problem are we trying to solve with AI.' It's like, what are the boundary conditions where AI is responsible versus irresponsible given the underlying nature of the technology?"

The question "what should we automate" differs fundamentally from "what can we automate." The default path answers only the second.

Choosing the Slower Path

This series offers frameworks for leaders who want to choose deliberately rather than follow by default. Each subsequent article addresses a specific dimension of intentional human-AI collaboration.

Part 2 examines where problems should be framed before AI engages them. Most AI deployments fail because organizations optimize at the wrong level of abstraction, asking AI to execute before they define what outcome they seek. Research suggests 80 to 95 percent of AI project failures stem from this wrong-level optimization. A framework called the Problem Abstraction Ladder distinguishes human territory from AI territory, showing why the upper rungs demand human judgment regardless of AI capability. Leaders who skip these rungs guarantee failure regardless of how sophisticated their AI becomes.

Part 3 explores what AI cannot see. Holstein's research on "unobservables" reveals a consistent pattern across domains from child welfare to healthcare to education: AI models know only what their training data contains. They miss the reasons behind patterns, the context that explains exceptions, everything that experienced practitioners weigh when making decisions. The Streetlight Effect describes organizations that look where the data is bright rather than where the answer lives. The article develops methods for mapping these blind spots before they cause harm.

Part 4 addresses how to design around capability gaps. Danzico's friction principle, Brandenburg's augmentation framework, and Franznick's evidence-driven prototyping converge on a common insight: productive human-AI collaboration requires intentional calibration. Seamless automation serves some contexts. Beautiful seams serve others. Wizard-of-Oz methods, where humans simulate AI capabilities before they exist, prove valuable precisely because they maintain human involvement during testing. The design challenge lies in knowing which approach each context demands.

Part 5 considers pace and timing through the lens of organizational layers. AI capabilities evolve in days and weeks. Productivity improvements manifest over weeks and months. Expertise develops over months and years. Professional fields reproduce themselves across decades. Human needs for meaningful work operate at evolutionary timescales. When faster layers impose their pace on slower layers, strain accumulates at the boundaries. Cultural debt compounds. The article develops a framework for recognizing these strains before they become organizational crises.

The Choice Before Us

The default path leads somewhere. Organizations can follow it and arrive at a destination shaped by friction reduction. Or they can choose their destination and design the path to reach it.

Brandenburg distinguishes automation from augmentation to clarify the opportunity. "Automation simply means replacing things we already do," she explains. "On the other side, we have augmentation. This is very exciting to me because we don't even know yet what activities and behaviors are even possible." Augmentation requires imagination. The default path requires only momentum.

Flynn sees the stakes clearly: "I do think there's going to be a new humanism emerging at work that we have to define. Because we all need work too. We need work. It's our identity. It's economic activity, it's our way of surviving."

The leaders interviewed for this series share a common recognition. AI creates genuine value. The technology advances inevitably. Embracing it thoughtfully beats resisting it futilely. What remains possible is intention: choosing which friction to preserve, which judgment to protect, which expertise to cultivate, which pace to honor.

This series provides frameworks for that choosing. Not frameworks against AI, but frameworks for the human work that makes AI deployment wise rather than merely fast.

The path splits here. One direction follows the default of friction reduction. The other requires deliberate choice at every step. Both lead somewhere. Only one leads where you decide.
