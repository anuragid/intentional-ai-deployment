# The Cost of Speed

## Prose (before embed)

Part 5 of the series: *We Are Choosing By Not Choosing*

The earlier articles in this series established four principles. Friction reduction always wins: people reach for whatever removes the obstacle in front of them. Problem definition precedes solution: the ladder has rungs, and skipping them produces failures. AI operates under structural limits: some information never reaches the system. Integration requires design: keeping humans in the loop demands intentional architecture.

Each principle addresses a different challenge. Together they raise a final question: what happens to organizations that move faster than they can absorb?

## The Order of Civilization

Stewart Brand spent decades studying how complex systems persist. His answer appears in *The Clock of the Long Now*, published in 1999. He called it pace layers.

Brand proposed that healthy civilizations contain six layers, each moving at a different speed. Fashion changes fastest. Then commerce, infrastructure, governance, culture. Nature moves slowest of all. The diagram is simple. The insight is not.

> "The fast layers innovate; the slow layers stabilize. The whole combines learning with continuity" (Brand, 1999).

Fast and slow need each other. Fashion generates novelty that commerce can exploit. Commerce builds patterns that infrastructure can support. Governance ensures that infrastructure serves society. Culture shapes what governance considers legitimate. Nature sets the boundaries within which all human activity occurs.

The relationship between layers matters more than the speed of any single layer.

> "The fast parts learn, propose, and absorb shocks; the slow parts remember, integrate, and constrain" (Brand, 1999).

When this relationship holds, systems adapt without breaking. When it fails, the results are catastrophic.

Brand offered a blunt example.

> "If governance is changed suddenly instead of gradually, you get the catastrophic French and Russian revolutions" (Brand, 1999).

The Soviet Union tried to restructure governance at the pace of commerce. The system collapsed.

The pace layers framework was designed for civilizations. It applies equally to organizations.

## Organizational Layers

Organizations contain their own pace layers. Individual capability changes quickly. A designer learns a new tool in days. Team productivity follows. Workflows adapt in weeks. Expertise accumulates more slowly. Deep domain knowledge develops over years. Governance structures move slower still. Hiring practices, accountability systems, decision rights: these evolve across leadership tenures. Professional identity persists across careers. What it means to be a designer, a strategist, a researcher. Purpose endures longest. Why this organization exists. What it serves.

AI operates at the speed of the fastest layers. It augments individual capability immediately. It reshapes team productivity within weeks. This speed is valuable. The question is what happens at the boundaries.

Ewan Duncan, Senior Partner Emeritus at McKinsey and former leader of McKinsey Design, offers a frame. "The big frames where humans are most effective are at the front end and the back end," he observes. "Front end in terms of defining what problem are you trying to solve? Where is the value? What's the experience you're trying to create?" (Duncan, personal interview, October 2025).

The back end matters just as much. "You need to change how people behave, how they interact, whether it is end customers or internal employees. And that is a human endeavor. Everything in the middle can be augmented through AI and made and accelerated a lot faster."

Duncan's frame maps directly to pace layers. The front end and back end are slow layers. They involve purpose, identity, change management. The middle is fast. AI accelerates the middle beautifully. But the middle sits between layers that cannot move at the same pace.

## Technical Debt and Cultural Debt

Ward Cunningham introduced the concept of technical debt in 1992. He was building financial software and needed a metaphor his business colleagues would understand.

> "Shipping first time code is like going into debt. A little debt speeds development so long as it is paid back promptly with a rewrite... The danger occurs when the debt is not repaid. Every minute spent on not-quite-right code counts as interest on that debt" (Cunningham, 1992).

The insight was not that debt is bad. Debt enables speed. The insight was that unpaid debt accumulates interest. Eventually the interest overwhelms the original benefit.

Kelly Franznick, Chief Innovation Officer at Blink UX, sees this dynamic with his clients. "Most of our customers are large companies, so they by definition will have very fractured user experiences and they'll have a huge amount of technical debt. So it's hard for them to kind of reinvent themselves using these tools" (Franznick, personal interview, October 2025).

Technical debt constrains what AI can accomplish. But organizations accumulate another kind of debt as well. When faster layers force slower layers to change at inappropriate speeds, strain accumulates at the boundaries. Call it cultural debt.

Cultural debt works like technical debt. A little speeds progress. Shipping an AI tool before updating governance structures may be the right call. The danger is not borrowing. The danger is failing to repay.

Organizations that deploy AI at capability speed while neglecting slower layers are borrowing against their future. The interest compounds at the boundaries.

## Four Boundaries

Strain accumulates at four boundaries in organizational pace layers.

The first boundary lies between individual capability and organizational expertise. AI accelerates what individuals can produce. Expertise requires years to develop. Kathleen Brandenburg, Co-Founder of IA Collaborative and Lecturer at Harvard Graduate School of Design, describes the tension.

> "It's just like going to the gym when we're proxying information that's being proxied over and over and over again. Eventually our own brains, they atrophy. It's more difficult to write a Monday note today than it ever has been before" (Brandenburg, personal interview, October 2025).

The curation paradox from Part 1 reappears. You need expertise to evaluate AI outputs. AI removes the practice that builds expertise. The debt is manageable if organizations create alternative paths for building judgment. It compounds if they do not.

The second boundary lies between operational speed and governance structures. AI enables faster decisions. Governance evolved for slower processes. Donna Flynn, Vice President and Chief People Officer at Steelcase, raises the structural question.

> "How is the SEC going to hold machines accountable? How are shareholders going to hold machines accountable if a company stops making money and isn't profitable?" (Flynn, personal interview, Fall 2025).

The accountability structures assume human decision-makers. AI disrupts that assumption. The gap is not permanent. It is a lag that deliberate investment can close.

The third boundary lies between individual optimization and collective field health. Larry Keeley, Innovation Scientist and Professor at IIT Institute of Design, reflects on what AI would have meant for Doblin, the innovation consultancy he co-founded.

> "Doblin's had historically something like 380 employees... Ripples in the pond, how many different design firms were transformed and design practices inside of big organizations and teachers came out of it" (Keeley, personal interview, October 2025).

Had AI existed when Doblin was growing, the calculus would have been different. "None of that would happen if we'd had AI back then. I could have stayed with the original small group of seven or eight people. We could have done everything we were trying to do."

Individual firms optimizing individually would have produced less collective field development. The effects are visible today. "Nobody's hiring their students all of a sudden."

The fourth boundary lies between operational efficiency and meaning. Work means something to the people who do it. AI can expand what practitioners accomplish or narrow it. Flynn articulates what is at stake.

> "We all need work. It's our identity. It's economic activity, it's our way of surviving" (Flynn, personal interview, Fall 2025).

Organizations depend on practitioners who care about their work. Cultural debt accumulates when automation erodes the sources of that meaning faster than new sources emerge.

## Paying Down the Debt

Brand's pace layers framework carries a prescription. Each layer should operate at its own pace. Fast layers propose. Slow layers dispose. Healthy systems respect both tempos.

The organizations that thrive with AI will not avoid debt entirely. They will recognize what they owe and pay it down deliberately.

This means creating practice opportunities that AI does not displace. It means identifying governance gaps and investing in structures that evolve. It means maintaining hiring and training even when AI makes them less immediately necessary. It means ensuring that what remains after automation engages the sources of meaning.

Franznick captures the disposition this requires. "I myself fight to remain optimistic. That can be hard sometimes. So we look for other people who are opportunity seeking in that area" (Franznick, personal interview, October 2025).

Flynn names what this could become. "I do think there's going to be a new humanism emerging at work that we have to define, that you and your generation are going to really have to define, what is that new humanism?" (Flynn, personal interview, Fall 2025).

The pace layers teach that the question is not whether to accelerate. Fast layers should move fast. The question is whether the slower layers receive the investment they require to absorb what the fast layers produce.

## The Series Conclusion

This series began with a thesis: we are choosing by not choosing. Default adoption produces outcomes no one selected. Intentional deployment produces outcomes worth wanting.

Part 1 established the mechanism. Friction reduction always wins. Technology that removes obstacles spreads regardless of downstream effects. The first article named the pattern and its dangers.

Part 2 identified the first corrective. Climb the ladder before you build. Problem definition precedes tool selection. Organizations that skip rungs join the eighty percent that fail.

Part 3 mapped the structural limits. AI cannot see what never enters the data pipeline. Information access boundaries are stable ground. They determine where human judgment stays essential.

Part 4 offered design patterns. Integration matrices. Friction calibration. Beautiful seams. Keeping humans in the loop requires architecture, not aspiration.

Part 5 adds organizational tempo. AI is not the threat. Speed is not the enemy. The danger lies in deploying capability-layer tools as though they had no implications for slower layers. The opportunity lies in understanding your own rhythms well enough to invest appropriately at each level.

Humans remain essential at the front end and back end. Defining what problem to solve. Changing how people behave. Everything in the middle can accelerate.

The organizations that lead will deploy AI at capability speed while investing at governance speed, at profession speed, at purpose speed. They will capture the gains of acceleration. They will pay down the debt of speed.

The choice remains yours.
